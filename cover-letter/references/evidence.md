# Evidence for the cover-letter rules

Compiled 2026-10-28 from 17 sources (`sources.md`). Every quote below was checked by string match against a saved copy of its source. Some quotes are shortened to avoid dashes; none are reworded.

## Coverage and gaps

- University career offices: Berkeley (two pages, including a faculty-side account of screening), Yale OCS, Northwestern, UConn, UC Davis postdoc office, Cornell postdoc office (mostly non-academic letters), Toronto. UK recruitment: jobs.ac.uk (academic cover letter, supporting statement), University of Reading. Career columns: Inside Higher Ed (2013, 2016, 2020), Chronicle of Higher Education. Physicists and mathematicians: Amanda Peet (Toronto physics, search-committee notes), Niven Achenjang (mathematics postdoc advice).
- Most sources target faculty posts in the US, several in the humanities. Postdoc-specific material is thinner (UC Davis, NPA, MIT, Achenjang). Physics-specific material is two short notes.
- Not retrieved: NIH OITE cover-letter guide, King's College London supporting-statement guidance, an astronomy job-application guide from Swinburne (all failed to download).

## How much the letter weighs

- Peet (physics search committees): "I found cover letters to be essentially irrelevant, and any horn-tooting pretty annoying." and "do get the name of the University, the specific name of the Department, and the Chair’s name correct!"
- Achenjang (mathematics postdocs): "Each application will need a separate cover letter. Typically, these can be rather short."
- Inside Higher Ed 2013 (humanities faculty): "The job application letter, or the cover letter, is the most important part of your application."
- Berkeley, faculty view of first screening: "The primary criteria at this point are whether a candidate’s fields match their interpretation of the job description".

## Purpose: interpret, do not list

- UC Davis: "A cover letter connects your CV to the job. It should explain why this position, why this institution, and why you are a strong fit." and "It should not repeat the CV line by line."
- Toronto: "The cover letter does not simply summarize your CV; instead, it interprets and contextualizes your experiences, highlighting their relevance to the position".
- Berkeley: "The cover letter should not simply repeat the information contained in the CV; rather it should elaborate and frame the aspects of your academic training and background".
- Inside Higher Ed 2016: "If your cover letter reads like a list reiterating the information on your CV, it does not serve any purpose of its own."
- Inside Higher Ed 2020: "Don't try to describe each and every paper you've written in the cover letter. Instead, highlight one or two given papers and discuss their arguments and significance."

## Opening

- Berkeley: "Be sure to identify the position you are applying for by rank and sub-field in the first sentence or two."
- UC Davis: "State the position you are applying for and give a clear one-sentence summary of who you are."
- Northwestern: "this paragraph gives the letter’s thesis statement, clarifying how your mix of experience makes you the best candidate for the job."
- Toronto: "This paragraph should be direct and no more than three to four sentences."

## Research for readers outside the subfield

- Berkeley: "You need to describe the forest in which your tree resides, and explain why it matters in terms of the broader trends and issues within your discipline."
- Inside Higher Ed 2020: "Remember that members of the search committee are likely not in your subfield."
- Inside Higher Ed 2013: "This does not mean you should dumb things down, but that you should fully explain yourself, defining any specific terms you need to use".
- Toronto: "The research statement provides detail" and "the cover letter provides direction."

## Contribution, not only wishes

- UC Davis: "Do not write only about what you want; show what you can contribute."
- Cornell: "Emphasize what you can do for the employer, not what you hope to gain."
- jobs.ac.uk: "Explain why you want the job and what you can uniquely bring to the role. This latter part is really important."

## Specific fit

- Toronto: "Hiring committees can easily recognize letters written for multiple institutions, and these typically fail to demonstrate the sense of fit committees are looking for." and "Tailoring does not require reshaping your academic identity for every application."
- Inside Higher Ed 2020: "If the job ad states that the position is for someone who studies religion, for example, say you study religion."
- Yale OCS: "Mention specific faculty members, research centers, or programs at the institution that align with your work."
- UC Davis: "Do not use generic praise like “your prestigious institution” without specifics."
- Berkeley, on a profile that differs from the ad: "By doing so you can turn a question mark into an advantage."
- Berkeley, faculty view: candidates are dropped when the reader cannot connect them, "unless the committee member is unable to make a plausible connection between your letter/vitae, and their interpretation of the department’s needs."

## Evidence over adjectives

- UC Davis: "Use specific evidence rather than general claims."
- Inside Higher Ed 2016: "Show it through examples, rather than relying on clichés or platitudes."
- Inside Higher Ed 2020, on claiming to be uniquely qualified: such a claim "is very likely to be untrue."
- jobs.ac.uk: "Rather than saying ‘extensive experience’ say, for example, ’10 years’ experience’."
- Berkeley: "note the recognition it has received in the form of competitive grants, awards, publications in refereed journals, and/or presentations at major conferences."

## Tone

- UConn: "Write your letter with the mindset of being a future colleague and not as a graduate student or postdoc trainee." and "Every word counts and the words you choose can convey doubt or confidence!"
- Chronicle, on effusive language and praise of advisers: "Such writing tics make you sound like a student and undermine your projection of yourself into the faculty role."
- Toronto: "The tone should be professional, precise, and measured. Avoid promotional language, exaggerated claims, or overly personal framing."
- Cornell lists as a mistake: "Begin too many sentences and paragraphs with “I.”"

## Honesty and own role

- jobs.ac.uk: "It may be tempting to exaggerate, but this is likely to unravel if you are invited to interview."
- University of Reading lists as unacceptable: "Exaggerating, fabricating or misrepresenting your qualifications or experience".
- Berkeley, what committees ask: "to what extent is the candidate’s successful “track record” due to their independent capabilities vs. their PI’s vision."

## Consistency with the other documents

- UConn: "Be strategic with the content you include in your letter, providing new or additional information to complement the content in your other application materials."
- Northwestern: "the cover letter should not directly lift content from other supporting material."
- UC Davis: "The letter works best when it connects your CV, research statement, teaching statement, and future goals into one coherent story."

## Logistics and following the call

- Inside Higher Ed 2016: "Don’t submit materials that weren’t requested in the job ad, and don’t send a cover letter that exceeds the specified length." and "you don’t stand much of a chance if University of X ends up with a letter in which you discuss how much you’d love to join the faculty at University of Y."
- UConn: "Follow any specific instructions on page length. If they want up to two pages do not submit three!" and "If the job description does not identify a specific person to whom you should direct your application, address the letter to the “Search Committee.”"
- Northwestern: "double check that the cover letter is signed and saved as a PDF (preferably on Northwestern letterhead)."
- Berkeley: "Wrap it up quickly." UC Davis: "End with a confident and polite closing. Mention the enclosed materials and interest in further discussion."

## Length

- Toronto: "Academic cover letters are typically one to two pages, single‑spaced. Early‑career candidates usually fall closer to one page".
- Achenjang (postdocs): "Typically, these can be rather short."
- Inside Higher Ed 2013 (humanities faculty): "It should be no more than two pages, but definitely more than one and a half."

## The applicant's own voice

- jobs.ac.uk: "Don’t be tempted to use AI to write your cover letter for you".
- Chronicle: "We therefore recommend exercising restraint in using AI to write your cover letter."

## UK supporting statements

- University of Reading: "Use the criteria as headings to signpost what you are writing about."; "Failure to demonstrate alignment with 'essential' criteria can significantly diminish the likelihood of being shortlisted for an interview."; "Each section of the Supporting Statement has a limit for 4000 characters (not words)."; "If you do not have direct experience, you can use transferrable skills gained from outside of a work environment."
- jobs.ac.uk (supporting statement): "You have the opportunity to use sub-headings, ideally linked to those used in the person specification, to organise your writing." and "It is crucial to back up any of your claims with evidence".
- jobs.ac.uk (cover letter): "Make sure that you provide some evidence for all the essential elements as a minimum."

## Process

- Berkeley: "You may want to customize the letters for the three to five jobs most attractive to you."
- UConn: "Invite individuals who do not know you well to read your letter and ask them to share what they learned about you."
- Inside Higher Ed 2016: "leave yourself at least several days before a deadline to proofread and finalize your cover letter."

## Conflicts between sources

| Issue | Side A | Side B | Decision in SKILL.md |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weight of the letter | Humanities faculty guidance: the most important document | Physics committee member: essentially irrelevant, but errors are punished | Short, specific, error-free; scientific case carried by the research statement |
| Length | Faculty letters: one and a half to two pages | Postdocs and early career: about one page, "rather short" | One page for postdoc and research-associate posts unless the call asks for more |
| Naming faculty | Yale OCS: mention specific faculty and centres | Name-dropping without a real link reads as generic (Toronto, UC Davis on generic praise) | Name people only with a concrete scientific connection |
