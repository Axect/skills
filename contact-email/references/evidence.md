# Evidence for the contact-email rules

Compiled 2026-10-28 from 16 sources (`sources.md`). Every quote below was string-matched against a saved copy of its source.

## Coverage and gaps

- Funders: Humboldt Foundation (information for hosts of the Humboldt Research Fellowship, general advice page), MSCA ("6 steps" for postdoctoral fellowships). University postdoc and career offices: Waterloo (finding a supervisor), Caltech writing center (emails to professors), Stanford advising (emailing faculty), MIT Communication Labs (CEE: finding a postdoc; BE: professional emails), UW and Pitt (letters of recommendation), Yale fellowships (requesting letters), Auburn (contacting faculty sponsors), University of Oregon (postdoc application email). Associations and columns: ASBMB (cold emails for postdocs), National Postdoctoral Association, Nature (introduction only).
- Many etiquette sources address students; their timing and tone rules transfer, their content rules do not. Little physics-specific material.
- Not retrieved: UCSF sample postdoc emails, MIT-WHOI faculty-contact tips, a lab blog on cold emails (blocked). Duke's follow-up page yielded no extractable advice.

## Ask first, one ask

- MIT BE: "People often skim or don’t finish entire emails, so putting the request in the middle or at the end of an email risks our recipient missing the purpose altogether."
- Caltech: "Consider writing a short initial paragraph that explains who you are (if necessary)" and "Try to put yourself in the email recipient’s position and imagine what they need to know in order to make the decisions your query requires them to make".
- Stanford: "Your email should focus on a specific item, such as setting up a time to meet or informing your professor of an upcoming absence." and "When you reach out to a new professor that you want to connect with, your goal should be to set up a time to meet and talk in person."
- MIT CEE: "Make it clear who you are, why you are emailing, and what you want. Be polite, enthusiastic, and direct."
- Waterloo: "It is important to understand what you are asking for so you can be clear in your communication."

## Length and format

- ASBMB: "PIs are busy and get a lot of emails" and "Clarity and brevity are key."
- Stanford: "Long emails often get ignored until the recipient has time to deal with them, and faculty are extremely busy."
- University of Oregon: "Emails should be no more than four sentences."
- Caltech: "Use paragraphs to separate ideas, and keep paragraphs brief". MIT BE: "Numbered bullet points are particularly useful for emails which contain multiple requests."
- Nature: messages should be "shorter, simpler and more actionable".

## Salutation and formality

- Stanford: "If you're emailing a professor for the first time, it's better to err on the side of being too formal rather than too casual." and "Address your recipient by title and last name (*Dear Professor Interesting*)".
- ASBMB: "generally addressing the PI as “Dr. Smith” for example, is preferable to addressing them as “Jane” or “Sir” on the first email."
- MIT BE: "A cold email to a Professor should be formal, while an email to a professor you know well may be more casual and short."
- Waterloo: "Use proper letter format, formal salutations" with title and last name, "and your full name and contact information".

## Subject line

- Stanford: "Give a useful subject line (*Research on X*)".
- Caltech: "“Checking in” is much vaguer than “Seeking your response to my proposal.”"

## Why this person

- ASBMB: "It is essential to show you read their work and aren’t copying and pasting emails to various PIs." and "Pay attention to both small details, such as the spelling of the PI’s name and the institution’s name, and major details, such as what organism they work on".
- Waterloo: "Tailor your email to each faculty member instead of using a generic email" and "your goal is to stand out and demonstrate how your research and interests are a good fit with the research the faculty member is engaged in."
- MIT CEE: "It is better to send compelling emails to a relatively small list of scientists that you would be excited to work with than to blitz out the same exact email".
- NPA: "Why you are interested in the lab (Be specific!! Show that you have researched the lab and the type of work they do)".

## What you bring and the CV

- ASBMB: "This is the main focus of your email, the meat of it! Why should they hire you?" and "You should attach your CV, but your email should tell them what they really need to know."
- Waterloo: "if your PhD is not yet complete, ensure you provide information on when you anticipate completing all degree requirements".
- University of Oregon: "In case you are interested, here is my website, and I am attaching my CV to this email as well."

## State the cost of the ask (hosting, funding, fellowships)

- Waterloo: "If you already have funding, specify the source, value, and duration"; "If you are looking for endorsement for a funding application, provide details about the funding opportunity, including value, duration, and relevant application"; "If you are looking for a supervisor to fund your postdoc, ask if they currently have any funding to recruit or hire postdocs".
- NPA: "If you are interested in applying to fellowships, you should mention this when you reach out to potential advisors."
- Humboldt (to hosts): "First, applicants must coordinate their independently developed research proposal with you." and "It is therefore important that the applicant has developed the research proposal her- or himself."
- Humboldt: "Please only accept applications that you fully support and consider competitive in light of the applicant’s previous research opportunities." and "If the applicant intends to spend their research stay at two host institutes, separate host’s statements and confirmations of research facilities are required."
- MSCA: "To draft a successful application, you will need to work together with your prospective supervisor." and "It is therefore important to ensure that they can dedicate sufficient time to support your application and, if successful, your project."

## Referee requests

- UW: "You should be confident that the individuals you ask to write letters can write positive letters on your behalf. If you are unsure, ask them directly." and "give your references 2-4 weeks to write, edit, and send each letter. More than 4 weeks will be appreciated."
- Pitt: "Make sure they feel comfortable writing a strong letter."; "Professors receive many requests, so be professional, prepared, and give plenty of notice (at least 4-6 weeks)."; "Send a reminder two weeks before the deadline."
- Caltech: "you should indicate what the recommendation is for (with a relevant link to application requirements or a job ad if possible) and when it is due."
- Yale: "A copy/draft of your personal statement, or fellowship proposal."; "You may ethically provide a list of bullet points you would like the letter to address and/or a factual narrative of key achievements (avoid adjectives)"; "Write your recommenders and language evaluators a note of thanks" and let them know the outcome.

## Tone

- Caltech: "directness in English academic communication does not mean rudeness; it signals clarity, confidence, and professionalism." and "a simple “Thank you for your time” is enough."
- ASBMB: "There are ways to be direct and clear and still be polite!" and "Be enthusiastic and clear that you’d love a response" without being "pushy".
- Caltech: "Do not write that “I am bumping this to the top of your inbox,” as some professors will find this to be overly familiar or rude."
- Caltech, on generated text: "Emails composed this way will lack your voice and may give the recipient the sense that you are not truly engaged in the topic".

## Timing and follow-up

- Caltech: "Avoid sending an email at the very last minute and expecting an immediate reply." and "Give at least 2 business days before assuming your email was overlooked."
- MIT BE: "For non-urgent emails, waiting 5-10 business days to send a follow-up is standard. For urgent emails, waiting 2-3 business days may be more appropriate."; "When working with people in other time zones, try to send emails during the working hours of their time zone."; "A follow-up email should be short and polite, reminding your recipient of your core request."
- Stanford: "If you haven't heard back from your faculty member in about a week, it is reasonable to reach out one more time." and "The professor might be particularly busy that week, or out of town, or simply missed your message in a flood of other emails. Try to reach out again."
- University of Oregon: "Interest emails have a higher probability of effecting the outcome of the search if they are sent before the official deadline."

## Outcomes

- Waterloo: "consider how you can keep the door open for future possibilities, such as potential collaborations."
- Humboldt (to hosts): if an application is weak, "we would be grateful if you could communicate this to the applicant as openly as possible."
